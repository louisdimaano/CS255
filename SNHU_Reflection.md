CS-255 System Analysis and Design Portfolio
This repository holds two artifacts from CS-255: the Business Requirements Document from Project One and the System Design Document from Project Two. Together they walk through the entire process of analyzing and designing a system for a real-world style client, from first figuring out what they needed to handing off a design a development team could actually build from.

Project Reflection
The Project
The client was DriverPass, a driver training company started by an owner named Liam. The problem they were trying to solve was a real one: a lot of people fail their DMV driving test, and DriverPass saw an opportunity to fix that with better preparation. They wanted a system where students could take online practice tests, work through course material, and book on-the-road driving lessons with assigned instructors. On top of that, the system had to live in the cloud, work from any device, handle different kinds of users from customers to staff to the owner, and stay synced with the DMV so the practice material never went stale. My role was to take all of that, capture it in a requirements document, and then turn it into an actual system design built around UML diagrams.

What Went Well
The thing I am most happy with is how closely I kept the whole design tied to what the client actually said. It would have been easy to fill in gaps with assumptions, but instead I kept going back to the interview transcript so that every requirement and design choice traced back to a real request from Liam or Ian. I also think the UML diagrams turned out clean, especially the class diagram and the activity diagrams, where I managed to keep the notation correct without letting the diagrams get cluttered or hard to read.

What I Would Revise
If I went back to one thing, it would be the use case diagram, specifically how I handled the external systems. The DMV and payment processor were in there, but they were not fleshed out as much as the human users. I would fix that by giving each external system its own clearly named use case, like a dedicated "Receive DMV Updates" connection, so the part those systems play is just as visible as everything the people do. It is a small change, but it would make the diagram tell a more complete story.

Interpreting the User's Needs
Reading the interview was about more than just writing down what Liam asked for. A lot of the time the real need was sitting underneath the request. When he said he needed to know who made, changed, or canceled a reservation, the thing he actually cared about was accountability, so I designed in activity logging and reporting to cover that. Then I carried those needs into the diagrams, turning them into things like the Appointment class and the scheduling workflow. The reason this matters so much is simple: a system can be built perfectly and still be useless if it does not solve the user's actual problem. The design only exists to serve the client, and the moment you forget that, you start building something that looks finished but does not actually work for the people using it.

How I Approach Design
I try to understand the problem before I reach for a solution. That means getting a real handle on what the user is trying to do and what limits they are working within before I design anything. After that, I like to look at the system from more than one angle, using process models to see how things flow and object models to see how things are structured, because each one shows you something the other hides. Going forward, I want to keep using UML as a way to explain designs to both technical and non-technical people, and I want to keep treating requirements gathering as the foundation everything else sits on. The biggest habit I want to carry with me is checking my understanding with the client early instead of designing in a vacuum and hoping I got it right, because catching a misunderstanding up front is a lot cheaper than finding it after everything is built.
